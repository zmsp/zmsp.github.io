## 1. The 80/20 Rule: Why Phrasebooks Are Holding You Back

You've probably heard that the top 1,000 most common Spanish words cover about 80% of everyday conversation. That's a great hook — but it undersells the actual opportunity.

Paul Nation's lexical frequency research shows that the **top 2,000 word families** provide roughly **97% lexical coverage** of everyday spoken discourse. At that point, you can carry a real conversation, infer unknown words from context, and start building fluency at a natural pace.

Phrasebooks can't get you there, and here's why: they give you *lexical breadth* — a surface-level exposure to many phrases — but zero *lexical depth*. When someone responds to your scripted "¿Dónde está el baño?" with a fast string of natural Spanish, the phrasebook gives out. You haven't learned how Spanish works; you've memorized a few shortcuts.

Lexical depth means understanding how a word behaves in context — what it collocates with, how it shifts meaning, when native speakers actually use it. That's what frequency-based vocabulary learning builds, and it's what phrasebooks skip entirely.

**The takeaway:** spending your time on the top 2,000 most common Spanish words is not a shortcut. It's the most direct path to real comprehension.

## 2. Essential High-Frequency Spanish Categories (and the Infinite Loop Hack)

Most Spanish learning resources bury you in conjugation tables before you can say anything useful. There's a better entry point: **anchor modals**.

### The Infinite Loop Hack

Three high-frequency verbs unlock nearly every need you'll have as a beginner:

| Anchor Modal | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Quiero** | I want | *Quiero un café* — I want a coffee |
| **Necesito** | I need | *Necesito ayuda* — I need help |
| **Voy a** | I'm going to | *Voy a tomar el autobús* — I'm going to take the bus |

The hack: pair any of these with an infinitive verb and you can express almost any intention without touching a conjugation table. `[Quiero / Necesito / Voy a] + [infinitive]` is a productive sentence every time. This isn't a cheat — it's how beginners naturally start speaking in every well-researched language program.

### Cognate Exploitation: You Already Know Hundreds of Spanish Words

English speakers have a hidden advantage almost no one mentions. Spanish and English share thousands of cognates — words with the same Latin root. Many follow predictable patterns:

- English **-tion** → Spanish **-ción** (*information → información, communication → comunicación*)
- English **-ty** → Spanish **-dad** (*university → universidad, liberty → libertad*)
- English **-ous** → Spanish **-oso/a** (*famous → famoso, delicious → delicioso*)

When you recognize a pattern like this for the first time, it's a genuine psychological win. You realize you're not starting from zero — you have a foundation to build on immediately.

## 3. The Retention Problem: Why You Keep Forgetting What You Study

Here's something frustrating that every language learner hits: you study a list of Spanish words one evening, feel confident, and then completely blank on half of them two days later.

This isn't a you problem. It's a memory problem, and it has a name: **the forgetting curve**.

Hermann Ebbinghaus mapped it in the 1880s and it still holds up: without reinforcement, humans forget roughly 50% of new information within a day, and up to 90% within a week. The traditional response — just re-read your notes — doesn't work because passive re-exposure barely slows the decay.

### How Spaced Repetition Fixes It

The solution is **spaced repetition** — specifically the SuperMemo SM-2 algorithm, the same method researchers and polyglots have relied on for decades, and the foundation TopWords is built on. Here's how it works:

Every word you study gets its own personal review schedule. Nail a word? It comes back later. Struggle with it? It comes back sooner. Over time, the app maps your memory and fills the gaps automatically — not by shuffling a deck, but by calculating the **exact day you're about to forget a word** and scheduling a review right before that happens.

The modern implementation of this idea is **FSRS** (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler), a data-driven successor to SM-2 trained on millions of real flashcard reviews. It models your *retention rate* and *memory stability* per word, so every review lands at the highest-leverage moment in your forgetting curve. You review less but remember more.

### Why use TopWords over Duolingo?

Paul Nation's **Four Strands** framework is a useful lens here. Effective language learning requires four balanced activities: meaning-focused input (reading and listening to comprehensible content), meaning-focused output (speaking and writing), language-focused learning (deliberate vocabulary and grammar study), and fluency development (practicing at natural speed with known material).

Many gamified apps concentrate almost entirely on translation tapping — a narrow slice of the third strand — while neglecting output and fluency development entirely. The result is users who can earn points but can't hold a real conversation.

TopWords is built for high-efficiency vocabulary acquisition (strand three), and it pairs naturally with the other three strands. Use it alongside conversation practice and Spanish content — shows, podcasts, books — and you're covering all four.

### Is there a difference between SM-2 and FSRS?

Yes. SM-2 (the SuperMemo-2 algorithm, developed in 1987) was groundbreaking and still works well. FSRS is a modern, data-driven successor trained on millions of real flashcard reviews. The key improvement is in how it models *memory stability* — FSRS more precisely estimates how long a memory will last before it needs reinforcement, which translates to fewer unnecessary reviews and better long-term retention.

The most effective path to conversational Spanish:

1. **Start with anchors** — internalize `Quiero`, `Necesito`, and `Voy a` and you can communicate real needs on day one.

4. **Use spaced repetition** — let SM-2 or FSRS schedule reviews at the exact moment your memory needs reinforcement.
